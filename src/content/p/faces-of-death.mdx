Sometimes I cry at the idea of war. Not at a movie about war, not at a documentary, just at the idea of it, the plain fact that it's 2026 and we're still killing each other. I don't know how else to say it: it's barbaric, and it's embarrassing. We carry supercomputers in our pockets, we can talk to a machine that talks back, and we still settle our arguments by blowing up each other's children. If you wake up as a human being in this world and that doesn't embarrass you even a little bit, I think that's worth looking at, deep inside.

What sits under the crying is heavy. It's desesperación, sadness, and grief all mixed together, and the thought that I'll probably live my whole life, and my kids will probably live theirs, in a world ruled by war and violence. That feels like insanity to me. I know it's not a sophisticated position. I'm not a historian or a strategist, and I'm sure people who study this for a living could tell me why it's more complicated. But I don't think you need credentials to feel that something is very wrong here.

I people-watch. Always have. And now, with the internet and cameras everywhere, I get to watch the faces of world leaders too, all of them. You can open Wikipedia and browse general after general, president after president, and I promise you it's not a fun task. Almost every one of them is a man. Very few women show up in those pictures, so I guess the modern world has been ruled by warlords. Excuse me, statesmen. Sometimes I wish we'd tried a matriarchy, but that's a topic for another essay.

I'm not studying these faces, though. I don't have a method. It's more that my body reacts before my head does, some kind of pattern matching I can't fully explain, and I feel it most in the photos where their eyes are exposed and looking straight into the camera. Something is off. It's eerie. Religious people might say they look like they sold their soul, but I'm an atheist, and I don't have that framework, or that kind of authority to judge anyone. All I have is the feeling, like the light behind the eyes went somewhere else. Sadness, death, genocide, wanton destruction, all of it sitting in one face. I could be wrong about every single one of them. I've started to think the eeriness isn't something I'm discovering in them so much as my own grief running into the people whose decisions keep it going.

So why do we still kill each other? I keep landing on money and power. Somebody profits, somebody gets to stay in charge, and the rest of us get handed a story about why it was necessary. I'm sure that's too simple. But every time I peel back the complicated version, money and power are sitting right there underneath it.

And whatever the reason, anyone who takes part in it, whether they pull the trigger or approve the mission from a safe, carpeted distance, has to carry two truths at the same time:

1. Life is sacred.
2. Life can be ended on command.

I've tried to make those two sentences live in the same moral universe and I can't do it. "Civilization" seems to manage it, but only by never saying both out loud at the same time. Life is sacred. Life can be ended on command. Pick one.

What really gets me is how old and how simple the rule is. In the Abrahamic world, which shaped a lot of the men in those Wikipedia photos, it's about as clear as a rule can be: thou shalt not kill. Plenty of modern translations say "you shall not murder" instead, which, if you think about it, is the loophole already sneaking into the translation. And then, in very small print, _\*Certain restrictions apply._ Self-defense. Justice. National security. "Necessary force," two words carrying centuries of blood. Get out of here, man. We kept the slogan and industrialized the loophole, and plenty of us, me included, have preached the rule and cheered its exceptions at one point or another. I'm not holding anyone to scripture, since I don't hold myself to it either. I just notice that the one rule almost every tradition agrees on is also the one we've built the most exceptions for.

Still, I keep wondering if anyone really gets away with it. Can a person kill, or order killing, and come out whole on the other side, just because the paperwork said it was allowed? I needed to know if I was crazy for feeling that they can't, so I went looking, and it turns out people have spent whole careers on that question. Dave Grossman's [_On Killing_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Killing) (1995) describes how hard militaries have to work to train ordinary people past their resistance to killing, and what that costs them afterward. Bessel van der Kolk's [_The Body Keeps the Score_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Body_Keeps_the_Score) (2014) is four hundred pages about the body holding on to what the mind won't. Drone operators develop PTSD at [rates comparable to pilots who fly manned aircraft](https://alert.psychnews.org/2013/04/pilots-show-similar-mental-health.html), some of them without ever leaving Nevada. I'm not a scientist and I don't want to stretch any of that into a theory about faces. It just makes me feel a little less alone in sensing that the killing leaves something behind, even when it happens through a screen, a signature, or a vote.

And before this turns into me judging generals from the cheap seats: I haven't killed anyone, and I don't want to. The deadliest thing I've ever authorized is a production deploy. But my face has its own ledger. I spent years saying one thing and living another, suppressing instead of feeling, keeping a cold-war peace that looked calm on the surface while everything churned underneath. I can see that season in old photos of myself, and it's not subtle. So I'm not looking at these faces from some clean place. Maybe that's part of why they get to me.

Which is why I can't look at these men with pure hatred. Los rostros del dolor, les digo yo. The faces of pain. The faces of sadness. The faces of defeat. The faces of weakness. The faces of fear. I don't see strength in them, no matter how many medals get pinned underneath. I see what it might cost a person to live that far from the one rule, decade after decade, with applause instead of help. And I keep wanting to ask every one of them the same thing: what do you value? I mean the thing you decided was worth more than a life. It's a question I'd better keep asking myself too.

These days the face I look at most belongs to my youngest. Nothing is written there yet. All softness, all signal, a ledger with no entries. I can't keep the world from writing on him forever, and I can't end a single war. But I can teach him the rule the way it sounds before anyone adds the small print. Thou shalt not kill. No asterisk. No restrictions. And when he's old enough to ask me why the grown-ups keep doing it anyway, I hope I'm still embarrassed enough to cry when I answer.
