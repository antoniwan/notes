I just finished [Dispatch](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2592160/Dispatch/) on PC and I have no idea how many hours it took me. I wasn't watching the clock, I was watching the Z-Team and weighing the choices, and scum saving now and then when a pick didn't land the way I meant it, before committing to what I actually wanted. That's a good sign, so I'm writing this one while it's still ringing in my head.

This is one player's opinion, on the same scale as my [Crimson Desert review](/p/my-crimson-desert-review-after-200-hours/). I'd give Dispatch an **8 out of 10**, a _"Great!"_, a **4 out of 5**, whichever scale you like. Why not a 9? Once I figured out the dispatch mechanic I had basically figured it out, and from there I was staying for the story. AdHoc Studio, founded partly by Telltale alumni, seems to trade mechanical depth for narrative depth. I respect the trade, but for me the mechanics didn't pull the same weight the writing did.

## What Dispatch Is

Dispatch is a narrative-driven game where you play Robert Robertson, aka Mecha Man, a superhero who has moved into the dispatcher's chair. Your team is the Z-Team, a roster of ex-supervillains trying, in their various and chaotic ways, to stay on the right side of things now. You assign them to incidents by stats, you watch the calls play out, you make choices between shifts, and the relationships you build or neglect shape who they become. To me it played like Invincible meets The Boys with a tactical management layer, and way more heart than that pitch sounds like.

## The Dispatch Mechanic

![Dispatch screen on PC: incident details and Z-Team assignments](/images/2026/dispatch-review/dispatch-pc-game-dispatch.avif)

The loop lives on the dispatch screen: read an incident, match a hero's stats and personality to the call, live with the outcome. About a third of the way in it stopped being a puzzle. I started reading the calls the way I think the writers wanted me to, and the screen turned into a values check. Who are you willing to send into a mess? Who are you protecting from the call that would break them? Once I got that, I stopped optimizing and started deciding who I wanted these people to become.

The game does keep adding to it. Later on, some dispatches come with extra layers where emotions and the relationships inside the team start affecting the calls. I won't spoil how, but it's super fun.

## Hacking, Honestly

The hacking minigame is simple, but the rhythm of moving the mouse and hitting the keyboard in time with the screen actually starts to feel like hacking (if you're a character in the movie Hackers). I think the team found a way to sell the act without making you learn anything about hacking. Very cool.

It's still thin, a beat between story scenes, and I liked it that way, because I'm not a fan of mechanics that keep yanking me out of the story and breaking immersion.

## For Me, the Writing Is the Game

If you stripped the dispatch screen out, I'd still call it the best superhero story I've played in years, and I only saw one branch of it.

The Z-Team did the work, and I got attached to people whether I meant to or not. Some are funny in the Invincible way, casual brutality wrapped in charm, and some are tragic in the Boys way, where the costume is a layer over somebody who got broken at some point. I found the dialogue sharp and the voice acting committed, and I kept noticing payoffs that had been planted two or three scenes earlier.

It felt like Telltale writing to me, in the best way. Superhero stories can lean Superman, can lean anime, can lean a dozen other directions. This one felt closer to a prestige TV show.

Days after the credits I'm still chewing on the worldbuilding: the factions, the news clips, the characters, the costumes, the powers, the decisions. I'm working through lore when I'm not even playing the damned game.

## The Mecha Man Arc Is the Heart of It

The spine I'll remember is Mecha Man. He starts locked into extreme focus. The cape is the identity, the mission is the identity, and there's no oxygen for anything else. Then over the run he loosens. He lives with the Z-Team instead of just leading them, he makes human mistakes instead of tactical ones, and the game lets him sit inside those mistakes long enough for them to matter.

The way I read it, heroism in this game means helping people, staying in relationship with whoever is standing next to you, and doing the right thing when the right thing is small and inconvenient. The kill, the power level, the lonely focus that grinds your humanity into nothing, none of that seemed to be the point. Watching him figure that out while I sat at the dispatch screen figuring out my own version of it was the most resonant part of the run.

I won't pretend I didn't see myself in it. The man who can't separate himself from the work is a version of me I know pretty well. The slow work of coming back to the table, the relationships, the mistakes, the present moment, that's work I recognize from my own life. Putting that question in front of me through a dude in a mech suit is a credit to whoever wrote him.

## The Choices and Scum Saving

I'll admit it: I scum saved a few times. Not to chase the optimal ending. I'd pick what felt like the obvious choice, get the exact opposite of what I was going for, reload, figure out what I was actually pointing at, and then commit. Is that cheating? I don't know. I'm calling it intent alignment. The choices branch in ways I didn't expect, and I'd rather spend two extra minutes making sure my action matches my values than walk away from a moment going "wait, that's not what I meant."

For the run itself I played the dispatcher I'd want to be: compassionate when it was earned, firm when it was needed, patient with the people still figuring themselves out. That's how I try to move through my real life too, and it made no sense to become somebody else inside the game.

## The Ending I Got (Spoilers Ahead)

I got what I'm calling the deadly ending. Invisigal (or Invisibitch, depending on the day) went full villain. It was expected and unexpected at the same time. Expected because I'd poured most of my attention into Blonde Blazer: secondary lead, my boss in the dispatch room, a superhero in her own right, and the one who got most of my conversation time. Unexpected because Invisigal was getting attention from me too, just not the sustained, consistent anchoring she needed. She slipped back into who she'd been, because nobody on the dispatch side was paying close enough attention to keep her pointed at who she was trying to become.

The Z-Team are ex-supervillains, so I read her turn as a backslide, the thing the whole game seems built around. The premise is that they're trying not to be who they used to be, and the dispatcher is one of the people keeping that effort alive. I let one of them slip, so that one's on me.

If I replay (and I might), I want to anchor Invisigal and let Blonde Blazer drift, just to see the mirror version of my run. That I'm even considering it is the best sign of replay value I can give.

## The Art and the Polish

I also found the game beautiful. A lot of frames looked like comic-book panels, the characters read clearly even in the busiest ensemble shots, and the animation and sound in the cutscenes carry the emotional weight. Zuleyka walked by once during a romantic scene, said "whoa, romantic," and kept walking. When a non-gamer notices the craft from across the room, I take that as a good sign.

As somebody who builds software for a living, I notice the quiet stuff too: transitions that don't break immersion, menus that feel like part of the world, no obvious bugs across my whole run, audio mixed right between dialogue, ambient, and score. It looked and sounded as good as the writing read.

I came in expecting a fun narrative game and left with a new reference point for superhero stories.

## Should You Play It

I'd recommend it if you care about story, character, and choices that actually branch, or if Invincible, The Boys, and the deconstruct-the-superhero stuff is your lane.

I'd skip it if you want mechanical depth. I found the dispatch loop satisfying, but not a brain teaser. To me the whole thing is a story machine wearing a management game's clothes, which is exactly what I wanted, but it might not be what you want.

A heads-up for City of Heroes veterans and anyone who came up on build-your-own-cape games: there's no character customization and no costume design, and the limited power-picking mostly feeds the dispatch sections, not the story. You play Mecha Man, and the role-play lives inside characters who are already written. I think that focus is part of why the writing landed so hard, since a known protagonist lets the writers commit to a real arc, but the trade is real and worth knowing going in.

It was worth my time. Thanks to AdHoc for showing me the Telltale DNA still has plenty of life in it.
