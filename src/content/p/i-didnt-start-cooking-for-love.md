I've been thinking a lot lately about how my home cooking brings my family to the table like a moth to a flame. We have such an enjoyable time together during our home-cooked meals too. Back in the day, cooking was really the only way to get our teenager out of his room. I'm already thinking about dinner and dinner-prep now, ecstatic and salivating.

I started cooking for something completely different though. One day I sat down to understand my spending habits and debts a bit better, and out of everything on that nasty Excel file, the biggest thing I could stop spending immediately on was food orders or eating outside of home. Uber Eats and going out to eat frequently were killing my savings, honestly. I stopped eating out and started cooking at home immediately. Luckily I was single by then, separated, and my decision was not going to affect the rest of the family (yet). There was nothing noble in it too mano, it was just capitalism putting a hand on my back and my pocket, pushing me into walking into my own kitchen, kind of annoyed about it at the beginning.

At the beginning there were mostly the basics, the staples. My boring chicken breast, a name that my eldest put on my chicken. I remember him saying to me "mano, ponle seasoning a esto, this shit is so bland". Fair. I had just started my cooking journey and chicken had always been hit-or-miss for me.

From that bland chicken forward, poco a poco comencé a cocinarlo todo. I started racking up serious hours, cooking absolutely everything. Also luckily, during this period I was also changing my living habits, all of them, literally, and I was on a wellness and healthy journey through weightlifting, and running, so switching to whole foods and home-cooked everything was just excellent timing. But yeah, all of this started because of this economy. I remember promising myself, if you want to eat cake, you have to cook it. So just like that, I was cooking everything and anything I could think of, starting from the basics and foundational recipes.

For that I had two books at first. Bittman's _How to Cook Everything_ for the general grammar of the thing, and Valldejuli for my own people's recipes, the classics. I opened these books constantly because I could not hold a ratio in my head then and I still can't. Valldejuli's is still there on the kitchen counter, kinda-hidden to not get flak from the missus, but it's there. The basics, which I think I've learned correctly, have stuck with me, my broken memory kept, and it kept it without me asking, the *why*s of cooking and the whys of the basics of cooking. Why you pat a chicken breast dry before it goes anywhere near heat. Why salt needs 15, 20, 30 minutes to do its actual job instead of sitting on the outside tasting like salt. Why 325°F gives a beginner a much wider margin than 400°F does, which matters enormously when your margin is the only thing standing between dinner and a rubber sandal. Why ajíes dulces are not optional and recao is not cilantro and a cube of sofrito out of the freezer means the caldero starts with an advantage before you've done anything.

Those basics stuck. The foundations stacked fast, ideas leaking into each other the way learning always does when you stay with one thing long enough.

Cooking is where I proved it with my hands instead of arguing it in my head. Heat is physics, right? It behaves a certain way and keeps behaving that way, and so does salt, and so does time, and so does fat, and once four or five things behave predictably you can guess at the sixth and be roughly right, and roughly right with a hot caldero in front of you and hungry people in the house is most of dinner. Somewhere around 8,000 hours in by rough math now, and I'd check that number before I bet money on it, but the idea holds: what those hours actually bought was not really the money. It was another Tuesday of arroz con pollo for my babies and ladies, and it was delicious, and it brought us together.

Another thing I love about cooking is the flow state. I'm just flowing through the kitchen with the ingredients, my preparations, cleaning up after myself, probably dancing along to some music, it could be Peeling Flesh, it could be Juan Luis Guerra, whatever, doesn't matter which genre, yesterday it was Coltrane, it's about the movement and flow of my body, I am going to spend so many hours in this kitchen next, I will make this a moment to enjoy, and with this frame I started stacking the hours and the hours and the flow during cooking started looking mechanical, expert, because that's what practice does to you. It's that intentionality of each stroke and movement. If you have salsa in the background the movements might have some 3 and 4s extra steps but the intentionality comes with the fluency. Fluency in anything means consistency, and consistency is the hours. The idea of a kitchen and eating home-cooked food always is super romantic but fluency in a kitchen has a very unromantic effort behind it.

As you go, chicken stops being hard to cook with, and each ingredient is studied with these hands. You'll know when you are there, when you finally feel fluent with that piece of butter. My cooking is nowhere near art or expert. I am a studied beginner, a caveman cook, but I can produce my recipes on a Tuesday when I'm wrecked or on a Sunday for guests, because of the stacking. Funnily enough, having sofrito sitting in cubes in the freezer honestly beats most of the productivity systems I've implemented at work.

I told somebody recently that my cooking is dead simple, and then, being who I am, I went and opened my own recipe notes to prove it. Well. There's a paragraph in there explaining that a chicken breast is a wedge and what butterflying is actually for. There's a note about pulling meat at 155 to 158 because carryover finishes the job. There's a whole section on freezing raw portions, and not forgetting to sharpie the dates on each. So yeah, granted, to me my cooking is dead simple now because repetition just makes complicated things feel simple from the inside. Dead simple, sure. I wrote a paragraph about wedges.

Even the music helps, and I mean that literally, not as some metaphor about rhythm and life. A song is a clock you can dance to. Put on a long Tool track and you've got a pasta timer that also hands you steps and movement while the water boils. Using specific songs as timers IMHO is the coolest timer idea ever invented, no? Nobody's going to put which song belongs to which recipe in a cookbook but I do this in real-time: Ænema for the short boil, Pneuma when you've got a while.

So yeah, this essay started with me bluntly complaining about cooking to save money, the most soulless motivator, and look where it landed. I could've ranted about not being able to go out to eat out of necessity, or complained about how fucking insane grocery prices are right now, but somewhere around the first juicy tender chicken breast I actually wanted to cook, my reason changed. The practice still has its desires and parameters, and requires a specific effort that is rough, the price is there. But the reason, and the reasoning, and the whys for the experience itself changed, and so I stopped cooking because of the money and necessity and kept cooking because the food is so fucking good.

A home-cooked meal is just different too. The tiniest mac and cheese, made by somebody who cared for 12 minutes, is going to get in your body in a way the purchased version probably never will.

Compare that to eating out. It's a coin flip for me now anyway. It's a 50/50 chance whether I spend the rest of the day bloated and regretting it. Most of what's out there is garbage, and yes, I know I sound like an insufferable person saying that, and I've eaten plenty of it happily so I'm not putting myself above anyone by saying this, it's just the resentment and contempt seeping out. Most of the junk food will fuck my shit up, it's a reality. I'm 39 now, I'm minimizing my FAFO.

I wasn't always this picky. I lived in Miami for over 12 years and ate at all sorts of restaurants, and the expensive ones with no gimmicks, just the food, were unbelievable in flavor. Funny to think I could afford them then, or thought I could, jaja. Those chefs run the same fundamentals I'm trying to run, except they've run them 10,000 times more than I have, in a clean room, with product that came in that morning. A cook who has broken down the same fish 400 times and is frankly bored of it, that's craft doing the work, and luxury is just what they get to charge for it.

A craft that started for me as a necessity became one of the real pleasures of my life, worthy of every hour I put into it. That inversion of purpose still surprises me right to this moment that I'm writing this. Cooking home meals and eating home-made food are simply some of the best things in this life and they must be shared as much as you can. Food is nurture and soul, to be shared as such. The caldero and the DIY were supposed to be budget instruments and they're now some of the best and tastiest parts of my week. I got pushed into the kitchen and I stayed for the delicious food and my family's smiles. I keep going because of what the food does to a house. I make cornbread, gluten-free, out of a bag, nothing heroic, and my family materializes out of the walls. I make the magic brownies, IYKWIM, and the adults in my life have a very pleasant evening and bring it up for a week afterward. My kids eat things I made with my hands. Es una bendición, de verdad.

So yeah, keep more of your money and pick up cooking because things just taste better and people will sit down at your table. Me voy a comer un arroz con pollo que cociné anoche.
